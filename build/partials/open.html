Orthogonal Ensembles and Tested Explanations for Performer-Independent Body-Motion Emotion Recognition

A contest to name the emotion a performer is acting, from motion capture alone. The gain comes from models that make different mistakes, and every explanation is a test that could have come back negative.

Naoto Nishida and Yoshio Ishiguro The University of Tokyo

MMAC Challenge at ACII 2026 · Oral

Good morning. I’m Naoto Nishida from the University of Tokyo, with Yoshio Ishiguro. The paper has two halves: an ensemble that works because its members disagree, and explanations we tested, each able to come back negative.

Reading emotion from body motion alone

Entrants name the emotion in a motion-capture recording of a single performer. The system sees 24 joints on the body plus one point for where the body is in the room: no face, no sound, no scene.

Orthogonal errors

Combining models pays only when they are wrong on different recordings.

Tested explanations

Every claim about what the model reads is a test that can come back negative.

Each recording is one performer acting one emotion; every model here reads a fixed 64-frame window of it, about half a second. The recordings come from DIEM-A, the Diverse Intercultural E-Motion Database of Asian Performers.

A performer acts one emotion, and the model sees only the skeleton. The twelve run from anger and joy to jealousy, shame and pride, all acted.

The split is the hard part. The 7,992 training recordings are balanced at 666 an emotion, and every test recording comes from one of eighteen people who appear nowhere in training.

Chance is 8.3 percent, and movement alone often does not settle which of the twelve emotions it is. Body-only is the point of the challenge, and it is also what makes it hard.

The setting is what makes this hard

Two people performing the same emotion differ about as much as one person performing two different emotions.

Close emotions move alike

Jealousy moves like contempt, and shame moves like guilt.

Every performer moves differently

The people in the test recordings never appear in training, so learning how one person moves buys nothing.

We retrained the organisers’ reference model, STGCN++, on this split: 25.73 ± 4.03% Macro-F1, against 8.3% for guessing. A better network on its own gains little here.

Every score here comes from 10-fold leave-performers-out cross-validation over the 74 training performers, reported as the per-fold mean ± SD.

Two things make it hard. Emotions close in meaning are close in movement, and everyone has a movement style of their own.

The second one is why we hold out performers rather than recordings. With the same person on both sides of the split, a model can score well by recognising them.

The organisers’ reference model reaches 25.73 there. We reran it ourselves on the same 74 performers under the same folds, which is what makes every later comparison protocol-matched. The official baseline is within one standard deviation of that reproduction, and it stays an outside anchor.

The single-model route ran out first

Every upgrade to a single model failed or made the score worse.

  • A supervised-contrastive head cost −11.53 pp.
  • Aligning recordings to scene descriptions cost −1.42 pp: the projection collapsed onto performer identity, not emotion.
  • Distilling a vision-language model failed: on faceless stick figures it ranked the true emotion last.
  • Post-hoc calibration moved the score by 0 to −0.14 pp.
30.05 ± 3.48% best single model Macro-F1, same split

All on the same ten-way performer split. The paper lists eight of these; together they are the ceiling on one better network.

We tried the obvious upgrades to a single model first. The contrastive head cost eleven and a half points, because with twelve emotions in a batch of 128 there are too few same-emotion pairs to learn from. Each of these ran under the same folds as everything else.

Eight attempts, the same pattern. We report them as constraints on the design rather than leaving them out. The best single model we could build stops near 30 percent, and that ceiling on one better architecture is what sent us to diversity.

Combine models that make different mistakes

Averaging cancels a mistake only when the members do not make it together, so the members were chosen for how differently they fail.

Capacity

More capacity in one network. Under this split it did not transfer to performers the model had never seen.

Diversity

Eleven models whose assumptions about motion differ, so the recording that defeats one family is not the one that defeats the next.

This can fail. If the gain came from parameters, the members’ errors would coincide and dropping one would cost nothing. Both are measured later in the talk.

So the design stops buying capacity and buys disagreement instead. The reasoning is narrow. If two models are wrong on the same recording, averaging them keeps the mistake. If they are wrong on different recordings, averaging cancels it. Parameter count does not enter that anywhere.

Different assumptions about motion break on different people, and that is something we can choose members for.

It is a claim that can fail, which is the useful part. The next two slides are what we chose and how it is combined.

Each family assumes something different about motion

One larger network was the alternative. The capacity went to these four assumptions instead.

The frozen branches disagree with the rest more than any other family, and removing that block costs −2.91 pp.

Seven members trained here plus four frozen branches with a linear head each: 12.98 M trainable parameters.

Eleven members in four families, and a family is in the pool for what it assumes, not for what it adds. The graph networks read a joint through the joints it is attached to. The attention model drops that and lets any two joints, at any two moments, count together, however far apart they sit. The hybrid MLPs pool joints and frames into statistics with almost no structural assumption. The frozen four were pretrained on generic motion corpora elsewhere, and only a linear head is trained here.

Those last four disagree with the rest more than any other family, and taking that block out costs 2.91 points. The whole pool comes to 12.98 million trainable parameters.

Equal weights, raw logits, nothing fitted

Add the eleven logit vectors and divide by eleven.

Why equal weights

A fitted weight is fitted to the performers in the training folds, which is the thing that does not transfer here.

Why raw logits

A softmax first bounds how far one member can disagree with the rest.

+1.15 pp logit mean over probability mean same members, same folds
+0.99 pp members z-scored first scale removed
+0.69 pp ranks only, Borda fusion magnitude discarded

The two smaller figures are the same comparison with the members rescaled and then with their magnitudes thrown away, so the gain is not an artifact of one member's logits being larger than another's.

Nothing is weighted, and that is deliberate. A fitted fusion weight would be fitted on the training folds, which under leave-performers-out means fitted to the 74 performers we happen to have. With nothing fitted, there is nothing in the fusion to overfit.

The second choice is the raw logits rather than the probabilities, worth 1.15 points. A softmax first bounds how far a member can disagree with the rest, and that margin is the part worth keeping. It is not an artifact of logit scale: z-score every member first and +0.99 survives, and fusing ranks alone, magnitudes gone, keeps +0.69.
