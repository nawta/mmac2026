Orthogonal Ensembles and Tested Explanations for Performer-Independent Body-Motion Emotion Recognition

A contest to name the emotion a performer is acting, from motion capture alone. The gain comes from models that make different mistakes, and every explanation is a test that could have come back negative.

Naoto Nishida and Yoshio Ishiguro The University of Tokyo

MMAC Challenge at ACII 2026 · Oral

Good morning. I’m Naoto Nishida from the University of Tokyo, and this is joint work with Yoshio Ishiguro.

The MMAC Challenge asks entrants to name the emotion in a motion-capture recording of a single performer. The organisers keep the test labels, so no entrant can score their own system on the test set.

The paper has two halves. The first is an ensemble that works because its members disagree with each other. The second is a set of explanations we tested, and every one of them could have come back negative. At the end I’ll show that both halves come from the same structure in the data. This runs about twelve minutes, and then I’m happy to take questions.

Tested explanations

Every claim about what the model reads is a test that can come back negative.

Each recording is one performer acting one emotion; every model here reads a fixed 64-frame window of it, about half a second. The recordings come from DIEM-A, the Diverse Intercultural E-Motion Database of Asian Performers.

A performer acts one emotion, a motion-capture system tracks reflective markers on their body, and what the model gets is the skeleton that comes out of it: twenty-four joints in three dimensions, plus one point for where the body is in the room. Nothing else, so no face, no audio and no scene. The twelve run from anger, fear and joy to jealousy, shame, gratitude and pride, all of them acted.

The split is the hard part. Seventy-four performers in training, forty Japanese and thirty-four Taiwanese, give 7,992 recordings, evenly balanced at 666 per emotion. The test set is 1,944 recordings from eighteen other people, nine Japanese and nine Taiwanese. Nobody appears in both, so every test recording comes from a stranger.

Chance is 8.3 percent and a strong baseline only reaches about 25, because the movement alone often does not settle which emotion it is.

The setting is what makes this hard

Two people performing the same emotion differ about as much as one person performing two different emotions.

Close emotions move alike

Jealousy moves like contempt, and shame moves like guilt.

Every performer moves differently

The people in the test recordings never appear in training, so learning how one person moves buys nothing.

We retrained the organisers’ reference model, STGCN++, on this split: 25.73 ± 4.03% Macro-F1, against 8.3% for guessing. A better network on its own gains little here.

Every score here comes from 10-fold leave-performers-out cross-validation over the 74 training performers, reported as the per-fold mean ± SD.

Two things make it hard. First, emotions that are close in meaning are close in movement, so jealousy and contempt look alike, and so do shame and guilt.

Second, everyone has a personal movement style, so the same emotion performed by two people can vary about as much as two different emotions performed by one person. That is why we hold performers out rather than recordings: if the same performer appeared on both sides, a model could score well by recognising the person.

On that split, the reference model the organisers give us reaches 25.73, plus or minus about four. Every later comparison uses that number, because we retrained it ourselves on exactly the same 74 performers. A single better architecture does not gain much here.

What did not work

Every obvious upgrade to a single model failed or made the score worse.

  • A supervised-contrastive head cost −11.53 pp.
  • Aligning recordings to scene descriptions cost −1.42 pp: the projection collapsed onto performer identity, not emotion.
  • Distilling a vision-language model failed: on faceless stick figures it ranked the true emotion last.
  • Post-hoc calibration moved the score by 0 to −0.14 pp.
30.05 ± 3.48% best single model Macro-F1, same split

All on the same ten-way performer split. The paper lists eight of these; they are why we stopped looking for one better network.

We did try the obvious upgrades to a single model. The contrastive head, which pulls same-emotion recordings together in training, cost eleven and a half points, because with twelve emotions in a batch of 128 there are too few same-emotion pairs to learn from. Aligning recordings against written descriptions of the scene ended up encoding who the performer was instead of the emotion. Distillation did not help either: shown a stick figure with no face and no scene, the teacher ranked the true emotion last.

The best single model we could build tops out at about 30 percent. We report the failures on purpose. They are the reason we stopped looking for one better architecture and went for diversity instead.

Combine models that make different mistakes

We average eleven models from four families with different assumptions.

Each family represents motion differently, so they fail on different recordings.

11 models in the ensemble four families
12.98 M trainable parameters one recording, 11 models

Nothing is fitted: every model counts the same, and we average logits rather than probabilities.

So the design is simple. Four families that assume different things about motion: graph networks, an attention model, hybrid MLPs, and some frozen external pretraining. Eleven models in total, about thirteen million trainable parameters, each reading the same half-second window, and we average their logits with equal weight. Nothing in the averaging is learned or tuned.

We are betting that the gain comes from the members disagreeing rather than from the extra parameters. The next three slides are where I try to show that the bet is right.
