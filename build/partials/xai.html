Explanations you can test

A picture of where a model looked cannot come back wrong. A test can.

The second half of the talk asks which parts of the body the system uses, and answers by interfering with the movement rather than by drawing a picture.

That was the first half of the talk: where the number comes from and why it works.

The second half is the one I care more about. When people explain a model like this one, they usually draw a picture: a body with each part coloured by how much the model leaned on it. The trouble is that a picture like that cannot come back wrong. A decorative one looks exactly like a real one, so nothing is at stake in drawing it.

So we built our explanations as a set of checks instead. Each one makes a claim about a part of the body, interferes with that part, and reports what happened, with a number and something to compare that number against. Five of them come back positive and one comes back negative. All of it runs on models we had already finished training, so nothing is retrained and the score does not move.

Here is the scorecard. The recipe is the same every time. We want to say the model uses some part of the body, so we interfere with that part. We hide it, we add noise to it, or we edit how it moves, and then we look at whether the answer changes.

That gives six checks, each with a value and either a null or an interval beside it, so any of them could have come back the other way. Five come back positive. One comes back negative, and it gets a slide of its own, because a check that can only come back positive is worth nothing.

Two things about scope. All of this runs on the finished models, after training is over, so it costs no accuracy. And each check runs on one strong model out of the eleven we submitted, with the two that carry the most weight repeated on all eleven together.

Everything after this depends on the first two checks.

The first asks whether the model uses the parts it points to. We sort the six body regions by how much the model leaned on them, hide them in that order, and compare that against hiding them in the opposite order. Accuracy falls further when the important regions go first. The gap between the two orders is 0.124, and it comes out positive in every one of the ten runs, each of which holds out a different group of performers. Split the body into all 25 tracked points instead of six regions and the gap grows to 0.199.

The second asks whether that ranking is a property of the model or an accident. Add a little noise to the motion, work the ranking out again, and it agrees with the first ranking at 0.98, where 1 would mean the same order. The region at the top never changes.

Without those two, the match I am about to show would just be a picture agreeing with itself.

The model reads the body the way a movement analyst would

Laban Movement Analysis is that analyst's vocabulary for how a body moves. People wrote it; nothing learned it from our recordings.

  • The regions the model uses rank the way the Laban terms do: ρ = +0.500, where +1 would be the same order.
  • Speed and acceleration reach +0.033, about 15 times weaker.
  • Sadness reads as a bowed, sunken posture: head down, trunk leaning forward.

One strong model of the eleven, four body regions. The twelve emotions are not independent, so the 95% interval [+0.150, +0.733] keeps each emotion whole, p < 0.001. Positive for 10 of the 12 emotions (sign test p = 0.039, median ρ = +0.70).

This is the main result. Movement professionals have a vocabulary for describing how a body moves, called Laban Movement Analysis: how far the head bows, how far the trunk leans, how high the body is carried. People wrote those rules down, and nothing learned them from our recordings.

So we have two rankings of the four body regions, which are head, arms, legs and torso. One ranking is how much the model leaned on each region. The other is what the Laban attributes say about that emotion. The two agree at 0.500, on a scale where 0 would mean unrelated and 1 would mean the same order. As a control we put the plain physical measures of the motion through the same test, per-joint speed, acceleration, range of motion and energy, and they reach 0.033. The Laban match is about fifteen times stronger.

We are careful with the statistics here, because the twelve emotions are not twelve independent observations. The interval keeps each emotion together as a block, and the null shuffles the regions within an emotion. The match comes out positive for ten of the twelve.

What I like about it is that it reads in plain language. For sadness the model uses what you would expect: the head bows, the body is carried lower, the trunk leans forward.

Two more checks. First, we run the whole thing again on all eleven models together, which is the system we actually submitted, and the match comes out at 0.517, a little stronger if anything, with the whole interval above zero.

Second, on the right, we stop hiding parts and edit the movement itself. Freeze the head, and 41 percent of the answers change. Amplify one arm, and 23 percent change. The parts come out in the same order as they did under hiding, so two different kinds of interference agree with each other.

The fourth number is the written accounts. The system puts each decision into words on a card, citing the regions and the Laban attributes that card rests on, and we went through fifty of them looking for a sentence the measurements do not support. We found none.

I want to be precise about what all this gives us. The edits support the finding. They are not proof of cause, and the main evidence is still the 0.500 against 0.033 match.

The negative we report

Inside the half-second of movement the model reads, every frame counts about the same. Across the body, the head and neck dominate.

The model reads a window of 64 frames, about half a second at 120 frames per second. Measured across frames, the gap between hiding in importance order and hiding in the opposite order is only about +0.002, against +0.124 ± 0.031 across body regions, and importance is spread across the frames 98.7% as evenly as it could be per recording, 99.5% averaged by emotion. Order still matters: playing the motion backwards moves accuracy, so this is a claim about picking out a frame.

This is the negative, and I think it is what makes the rest of this a test. Every model here reads a window of 64 frames, which is about half a second of movement. When we ask which frames inside that window matter, the answer is all of them, roughly equally.

Importance is spread across the frames about 98.7 percent as evenly as it possibly could be, and hiding the frames in importance order costs almost nothing more than hiding them in the opposite order, where the same measurement across body regions gave a clear positive. On the right you can see the other side of it: the head and neck dominate in all twelve emotions. The evidence is spread through time and concentrated in space.

To be precise, this is about picking out a single frame, not about time in general. Order still matters. Play the motion backwards and accuracy moves. We report the null rather than hiding it.

If someone asks about the fall at the ends of the curve: importance tapers over roughly the last eight frames and the first one, from about 0.018 down to 0.004 against a flat level of 0.0156. It is the edges of the window that fall away, not any frame inside it, and the spread stays at 99.5 percent of the most even it could be. That is why the claim rests on the spread and on the near-zero gap rather than on the shape of the curve.
