Orthogonal Ensembles and Tested Explanations for Performer-Independent Body-Motion Emotion Recognition

Naoto Nishida and Yoshio Ishiguro

The University of Tokyo

What this work is

A challenge system that names one of twelve acted emotions from body motion, and two findings about why it works and which parts of the body it reads.

The task is to name one of twelve acted emotions from skeleton motion alone, with no face, no audio and no scene, and to do it for performers the model never saw during training. That last condition is the hard one. Emotions close in meaning share kinematics, so the model takes jealousy for contempt and guilt for sadness or shame. Every performer also moves in their own way, so the spread between people can be wider than the spread between emotions. Chance is 8.3%. A reproduced STGCN++ baseline reaches 25.73 ± 4.03% Macro-F1 under the 10-fold leave-performers-out protocol used everywhere on this page, over the 74 training performers (7,992 training clips, 1,944 test clips from 18 held-out performers).

The submitted system reaches 36.80 ± 4.00% Macro-F1, which is +11.07 pp above that reproduction on the same split, a relative gain of +43%. The gain comes from disagreement among the members. We combine eleven models from four families of inductive bias (graph convolution, attention, hybrid MLP, and frozen external pretraining) by averaging their raw logits, with no fitted fusion weights. Their per-sample error correlations sit between 0.15 and 0.43, so the members tend to be wrong on different clips, and averaging cancels mistakes instead of reinforcing them. Adding capacity to one model did not produce the same gain: the best single model we could train tops out at 30.05 ± 3.48%.

The second half of the work tests its explanations instead of drawing them. Whenever the system claims that the model uses a body region, we mask that region, perturb it, or edit its motion, and check whether the prediction moves. The suite returns six verdicts and we report all six: five positive and one negative. The central positive result is that region saliency lines up with rule-based Laban Movement Analysis attributes at Spearman ρ = +0.500, against +0.033 for a classical-kinematics control, roughly fifteen times stronger, and the same alignment holds for the submitted ensemble at ρ = +0.517. For sadness the model attends to the regions a movement analyst would name: a bowed head, lowered head height, and a forward trunk lean.

The work claims less than the numbers on their own might suggest. The headline is cross-validation on the labeled training performers rather than a leaderboard placement, and the hidden-test result is still pending. An absolute Macro-F1 near 37% is low, and we do not defend it as good. The Laban result is semantic alignment, and we test whether the model depends on a region separately, by masking that region and by checking the ranking under noise. The counterfactual motion edits corroborate that the model relies on that motion without proving causation. The difference between the Japanese and Taiwanese performers is a dataset stratum tangled up with capture era and individual movement style, and we do not read it as a cultural finding. The limits section states each of these with the check that bounds it.

Results

Every row below is scored on the same 10-fold leave-performers-out split, so the numbers compare directly.

The whole ensemble has 12.98 M trainable parameters. One inference is eleven forward passes over a 64-frame clip and then an average with no fitted weights. Averaging the raw logits rather than the probabilities is worth +1.15 pp on its own, and that gain does not come from the scale of the logits, since z-scoring the members first retains +0.99 pp and a rank-only Borda fusion retains +0.69 pp.

Scope 36.80 ± 4.00% is 10-fold leave-performers-out cross-validation on the labeled training performers. It is not a leaderboard placement, and the hidden-test result is pending.

Why the ensemble works

The members rarely fail on the same clip, and no single member carries the result.

Pairwise per-sample error correlations across the eleven members fall in [0.15, 0.43], and the lowest ones belong to the frozen external-pretraining branch, which differs most from the rest. Moderate correlation does not mean redundancy: the two MAMP members correlate with each other and still supply the largest single contribution. Removing the whole frozen external block at once costs −2.91 pp of pooled out-of-fold Macro-F1, so the gain is spread across the branches rather than coming from one of them.

The errors that remain fall where the task itself is ambiguous, between semantically confusable emotions: jealousy into contempt, guilt into sadness or shame. That matches a low maximum confidence of about 62%. Inter-performer Macro-F1 varies by roughly a factor of four, and that variation splits into about half intrinsic difficulty and about half error specific to an architecture. The architecture-specific half is what the ensemble cancels, and the explanations below examine that same disagreement among the members.

Explanations that were tested

Every explanation here comes with a test that could have failed, and one of the six did.

A saliency map that is only drawn cannot be wrong, since a decorative attribution looks the same as a real one. So we pair every claim with a check that can fail: mask a region and see whether accuracy drops more than it does for an unimportant one, add noise and see whether the ranking survives, edit the motion and see whether the prediction moves. The suite runs on a strong member of the submitted ensemble after training, costs 0 pp of Macro-F1 and needs no retraining, and we re-run its two most important checks on the submitted 11-way ensemble itself.

Faithful pass

Masking the parts the model calls important costs more accuracy than masking the parts it calls unimportant. The AUC gap is +0.124 ± 0.031 over six body parts, positive in every fold, and +0.199 at 25-joint resolution.

Stable pass

The part ranking survives input noise, at Spearman ρ = +0.983 ± 0.039 for σ = 0.02, and the top-ranked part never flips. If the ranking moved under noise, the alignment below would mean nothing.

Semantically grounded pass

Region saliency agrees with rule-based Laban attributes at ρ = +0.500 against +0.033 for the kinematics control. The emotion-block bootstrap CI is [+0.150, +0.733] and the within-emotion permutation null gives p < 0.001.

Behaviorally corroborated pass

Editing the motion moves the prediction. Freezing the head is the most disruptive edit (Δp_true = −0.0164, and 41% of predictions flip) and amplifying an arm flips 23.1%. Masking and motion edits are two different kinds of perturbation, and both rank the head and neck region first.

Grounded narration pass

Of 50 audited explanation cards, 0 contain a claim the underlying scores do not support. The cards are qualitative, so we report grounding for them and no accuracy number.

Frame localization reported negative

Frame importance inside a window is close to uniform. Per-sample entropy is about 98.7% of log T, the class mean is 99.5%, and the important-minus-reverse AUC gap is about +0.002 against +0.124 in space. The model's evidence is spatial, and no single frame carries it. This is a claim about locating frames and not about time in general, since reversing a clip does move accuracy.

On the submitted 11-way ensemble the Laban alignment is ρ = +0.517 (CI [+0.333, +0.700], p = 0.001) and the part-masking gap stays positive in all ten folds, so the finding describes the system we submitted and not only one member of it. The motion edits corroborate that the model relies on that motion without proving causation, and the agreement between masking and editing is itself only suggestive at ρ = 0.49 over n = 6, p = 0.33. The claim therefore rests on the 0.500 against 0.033 contrast, which has the sample size to support it.

What this work does not claim

Each limit below comes with the check that bounds it.

  • The headline is cross-validation, not a leaderboard result. 36.80 ± 4.00% is 10-fold leave-performers-out cross-validation on the 74 labeled training performers, and the hidden-test result is pending. Every comparison on this page uses the same split, so the +11.07 pp gain is protocol-matched even though the absolute number is a development finding.
  • We did not re-evaluate the official baseline. The challenge reports 25.21 ± 4.49% on the 92-performer protocol, which we quote as an outside reference point. Our own reproduction of 25.73 ± 4.03% sits within one standard deviation of it, and we measure every improvement against that reproduction rather than against the official figure.
  • The absolute score is low. Macro-F1 near 37% on twelve classes is not good in isolation and we never argue that it is. Against a chance level of 8.3% it is about 4.4 times chance, the gain is +43% relative to the matched reproduction, and all twelve classes improve.
  • Laban alignment is semantic, not causal. It shows that the attributions carry meaning a movement analyst would recognize. Whether the model depends on a region is a separate question, which we test by part masking (+0.124 ± 0.031) and by stability under noise (ρ = +0.983 ± 0.039).
  • Counterfactual edits corroborate rather than prove. The edits move predictions in the same part ranking that masking produces, which is agreement between two methods rather than a causal account. That agreement rests on n = 6 (ρ = 0.49, p = 0.33), so the claim leans on the 0.500 against 0.033 contrast instead.
  • The Japan and Taiwan difference is a dataset stratum. It is entangled with capture era and individual movement style, and we do not read it as a cultural effect. Performer country is decodable from the motion features at 69.8%, which is why we treat it as a confound. Dropping the seven earliest Japanese training performers moves pooled out-of-fold Macro-F1 by −0.44 pp (36.50% against 36.94%).
  • The explanation results describe two specific systems. They cover a strong member of the submitted ensemble and the submitted 11-way ensemble itself, not skeleton emotion models in general.

The negative results are part of the record too. Supervised-contrastive heads, mixture-of-experts fusion, scenario-text alignment, vision-language distillation and post-hoc calibration all failed or regressed under this protocol. We report them in the paper with their configurations, since they are the reason the design went after diversity instead of a better single architecture.
