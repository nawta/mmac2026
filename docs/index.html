Orthogonal Ensembles and Tested Explanations for Performer-Independent Body-Motion Emotion Recognition

Naoto Nishida and Yoshio Ishiguro

The University of Tokyo

The MMAC Challenge is a contest on cross-cultural emotion recognition from body movements, held at ACII 2026, the fourteenth International Conference on Affective Computing and Intelligent Interaction.

Talk slides Paper · ACII 2026 Workshops, link pending Cite this work

What the contest asked for

Name which emotion a person is acting, from the movement of their body alone, for people the system has never seen before.

Entrants in the MMAC Challenge are given motion capture recordings of people acting emotions, each one labelled with the emotion the performer was asked to act, and have to build a system that names the emotion in a recording it was not trained on. The organisers keep the labels of the test recordings to themselves, so no entrant can score their own system on the test set, and a leaderboard the organisers run decides the contest.

A machine that reads a body rather than a face is useful wherever a face is not there to be read: at a distance, in the dark, turned away from the camera, or in a setting where filming faces would not be acceptable.

The recordings come from the Diverse Intercultural E-Motion Database of Asian Performers, DIEM-A, published by Cheng, Tseng, Fujiwara, Schneider and Kitamura at ACII 2025. One recording is one performer acting one emotion while a motion capture system follows reflective markers worn on the body: a 57-marker Vicon rig for most of the corpus, and a 41-marker OptiTrack rig for the five earliest Japanese performers, none of whom appear in the training split used here. What reaches the model is the skeleton that comes out of that tracking, which is 24 body joints moving in three dimensions plus one extra node carrying where the body is in the room, so 25 moving points in total. There is no video of the face, no sound and no view of the scene. Recordings run at 120 frames per second and a typical one lasts about 845 frames, and every model on this page reads a fixed window of 64 of those frames, about half a second of movement (0.53 s), which is the window the organisers' own reference system reads.

There are twelve emotions to choose between: anger, contempt, disgust, fear, joy, sadness, surprise, jealousy, shame, guilt, gratitude and pride. Naming one of twelve by chance is right 8.3% of the time. Two things make it hard to do much better. Emotions close in meaning move alike, so a system takes jealousy for contempt and guilt for sadness or shame. And every person moves in their own way, so the gap between two people acting the same emotion can be wider than the gap between one person acting two emotions. The contest puts that second problem at the centre by splitting the performers 74 for training and 18 for test with nobody in both, which stops a system from succeeding by learning how one particular person moves.

Entries are scored by Macro-F1, which scores each of the twelve emotions on its own and averages the twelve results, so a system cannot do well by getting the common emotions right and ignoring the rare ones. It runs from 0 to 100%. The training split holds 7,992 recordings from 40 Japanese and 34 Taiwanese performers, 666 for each emotion. The test split holds 1,944 recordings from 18 performers, 9 Japanese and 9 Taiwanese.

No number on this page is scored against the organisers' test labels, since entrants do not have them. Everything here is measured on a stand-in built from the training performers alone: those 74 are divided into ten groups, and the whole procedure runs ten times, each time training on nine groups and testing on the group left out. One such run is a fold, and the arrangement is what the paper calls leave-performers-out, because the people in any run's test recordings never appear in its training recordings. It reproduces the condition that makes the contest hard, on data whose labels we hold. The hidden test result is still pending, so nothing here is a leaderboard placement.

What this work found

Combining models that make different mistakes did better than any single model we could build, and the account of what the model reads was tested rather than drawn.

36.80 ± 4.00% Macro-F1 of the submitted eleven-model system Mean and spread over the ten runs, each tested on performers held out of its training
+11.07 pp percentage points of Macro-F1 above the baseline we reproduced on the same split A gain of +43% on that baseline, and about 4.4 times chance, which is 8.3%
ρ = +0.500 vs +0.033 agreement between the body parts the model used and the ones a movement analyst would name Against a control of ordinary motion measurements, about fifteen times weaker; +0.517 for the submitted system itself

Retrained on the split above, the reference system the organisers supply, STGCN++, reaches 25.73 ± 4.03% Macro-F1. It treats the skeleton as a graph, with joints for nodes and bones for edges. The system submitted here reaches 36.80 ± 4.00%, which is 11.07 percentage points higher, a gain of +43% on that baseline, and roughly 4.4 times chance.

That gain comes from disagreement between models rather than from a better model. Eleven models are combined by averaging the raw score each of them gives each of the twelve emotions, the number a model produces before it is turned into a probability, which the paper calls a logit. Nothing in the averaging is learned or tuned. The eleven are built in four different ways, and that is the point, because models built differently make different mistakes. The paper calls the four families graph convolution, attention, hybrid MLP, and frozen external pretraining, the last of these being models trained beforehand on other motion data and left unchanged here. Across every pair of the eleven members, the correlation between which recordings they get wrong runs from 0.15 to 0.43, so they are usually wrong on different recordings, and averaging cancels mistakes instead of reinforcing them. Making one model bigger did not do the same work: the best single model we trained reaches 30.05 ± 3.48%.

The other half of the work tests its explanations instead of drawing them. Whenever the system says the model used a particular part of the body, we hide that part, disturb it, or edit its motion, and check whether the answer moves. Six such tests were run and all six are reported, five positive and one negative. The main positive result concerns Laban Movement Analysis, a vocabulary movement professionals use to describe how a body moves, in terms such as how far the head bows or how far the trunk leans, written by people rather than learned from data. Rank the four body regions by how much the model used them, rank them again by those written attributes, and the two orders agree at Spearman ρ = +0.500, on a scale where +1 is identical orders, 0 unrelated, and −1 opposite. A control built from ordinary motion measurements instead of the analyst vocabulary reaches only +0.033, about fifteen times weaker, and the same comparison on the submitted eleven-model system gives ρ = +0.517. For sadness the parts the model used are the ones an analyst would name for it: a bowed head, a lower head height, and a forward lean of the trunk.

The work claims less than those numbers might suggest on their own. The headline is the ten-run stand-in on the labelled training performers rather than a leaderboard placement, and the hidden test result is pending. A Macro-F1 near 37% is low, and we do not defend it as good. The Laban result says the parts the model used carry meaning; whether the model depends on them is a separate question, tested separately by hiding parts and by checking the ranking under noise. Editing the motion corroborates that the model relies on that motion without proving causation. The difference between the Japanese and Taiwanese performers is a dataset stratum tangled up with when the recordings were made and how individuals move, and we do not read it as a cultural finding. The limits section states each of these with the check that bounds it, and the glossary collects the terms.

Results

Every row below is scored on the same ten runs over held-out performers, so the numbers compare directly.

The whole system has 12.98 M trainable parameters. Answering one recording means running eleven models over the same 64-frame window and averaging their scores, with no fitted weights. Averaging the raw scores rather than the probabilities is worth +1.15 pp on its own, and that is not an artifact of one model producing larger numbers than another: rescaling every model's scores to a common spread first still leaves +0.99 pp, and discarding the scores altogether and combining only the order in which each model ranks the twelve emotions leaves +0.69 pp.

Scope 36.80 ± 4.00% comes from the ten-run stand-in on the labelled training performers, which is cross-validation and not a leaderboard placement. The hidden test result is pending.

Why combining the models works

The eleven models, which the paper calls members, rarely fail on the same recording, and no single one of them carries the result.

Taken pair by pair, the correlation between which recordings two of them get wrong falls between 0.15 and 0.43, and the lowest values belong to the models trained beforehand on other data, which differ most from the rest. Moderate correlation does not mean redundancy: the two MAMP members correlate with each other and still supply the largest single contribution. Removing that whole pretrained block at once costs −2.91 pp, so the gain is spread across the four kinds of model rather than coming from one of them.

The errors that survive fall where the task itself is ambiguous, between emotions close in meaning: jealousy into contempt, guilt into sadness or shame. That matches a low maximum confidence of about 62%. Macro-F1 varies by roughly a factor of four from one performer to another, and about half of that variation is how hard the performer is for any model while the other half depends on which model is looking. It is that second half the averaging cancels, and the explanations below look at the same disagreement.

Explanations that were tested

Every explanation here comes with a test that could have failed, and one of the six did.

A picture of where a model looked cannot be wrong, since a decorative attribution looks exactly like a real one. So each claim here is paired with a check that could fail: hide a body part and see whether accuracy falls further than it does for a part the model called unimportant, add noise and see whether the ranking of parts survives, edit the motion and see whether the answer moves. The checks run after training, on a strong member of the submitted system. They cost 0 pp of Macro-F1 and need no retraining, and the two that carry the most weight were run again on the eleven-model system itself.

Faithful pass

Hiding the parts the model called important costs more accuracy than hiding the parts it called unimportant. Plotted as accuracy against how much is hidden, the gap between the two curves is +0.124 ± 0.031 over six body parts, positive in every run, and +0.199 when the body is cut into all 25 tracked points instead of six parts.

Stable pass

The ranking of parts barely moves when the input is jittered. Before and after agree at Spearman ρ = +0.983 ± 0.039 for noise of σ = 0.02, and the top-ranked part never changes. A ranking that moved under noise would be an accident rather than a property of the model, and the alignment below would mean nothing.

Semantically grounded pass

The parts the model used agree with the written Laban attributes at ρ = +0.500, against +0.033 for the control built from ordinary motion measurements. Resampling whole emotions gives a 95% interval of [+0.150, +0.733], and shuffling the region scores within each emotion reaches an agreement this strong less than once in a thousand times (p < 0.001).

Behaviourally corroborated pass

Editing the motion moves the answer. Freezing the head is the most disruptive edit: the score for the correct emotion falls by Δp_true = −0.0164 and 41% of answers change, while amplifying an arm changes 23.1%. Hiding a part and editing its motion are two different kinds of interference, and both put the head and neck first.

Grounded narration pass

Of the 50 explanation cards we audited, 0 contain a claim the underlying scores do not support. The cards are qualitative, so we report grounding for them and no accuracy figure.

Frame localization reported negative

Inside the 64-frame window, no frame counts for much more than any other. The spread of frame importance reaches about 98.7% of what a perfectly even spread would give, 99.5% averaged within a class, and the equivalent of the faithfulness gap above is about +0.002 in time against +0.124 across the body. The model's evidence is spatial. This says no single frame carries the answer, not that the model ignores time, since playing a recording backwards does change accuracy.

On the submitted eleven-model system the Laban agreement is ρ = +0.517 (95% interval [+0.333, +0.700], p = 0.001) and the part-hiding gap stays positive in all ten runs, so the finding describes the system we submitted and not only one member of it. The motion edits corroborate that the model relies on that motion without proving causation, and the agreement between hiding and editing is itself only suggestive, at ρ = 0.49 over 6 body parts, p = 0.33. The claim therefore rests on the 0.500 against 0.033 contrast, which has the sample size to support it.

What this work does not claim

Each limit below comes with the check that bounds it.

  • The headline is a stand-in for the test, not a leaderboard result. 36.80 ± 4.00% comes from the ten runs over the 74 labelled training performers, and the hidden test result is pending. Every comparison on this page uses the same split, so the +11.07 pp gain is measured under matched conditions even though the absolute number is a development finding.
  • We did not re-run the official baseline. The organisers report 25.21 ± 4.49% on a wider arrangement that uses all 92 performers, which we quote as an outside reference point. Our own reproduction of 25.73 ± 4.03% sits within one standard deviation of it, and every improvement is measured against that reproduction rather than against the organisers' figure.
  • The absolute score is low. A Macro-F1 near 37% across twelve emotions is not good in isolation, and we never argue that it is. Against a chance level of 8.3% it is about 4.4 times chance, the gain is +43% over the matched reproduction, and all twelve emotions improve.
  • The Laban agreement is about meaning, not cause. It shows that the parts the model used are the parts a movement analyst would name. Whether the model depends on those parts is a separate question, tested by hiding them (+0.124 ± 0.031) and by checking the ranking under noise (ρ = +0.983 ± 0.039).
  • Motion edits corroborate rather than prove. The edits move answers in the same order of parts that hiding produces, which is two methods agreeing rather than a causal account. That agreement rests on 6 body parts (ρ = 0.49, p = 0.33), so the claim leans on the 0.500 against 0.033 contrast instead.
  • The Japan and Taiwan difference is a dataset stratum. It is entangled with when the recordings were made and with how individuals move, and we do not read it as a cultural finding. A classifier can tell a performer's country from the motion features 69.8% of the time, which is why we treat country as a confound. Dropping the seven earliest Japanese training performers moves the score on the pooled held-out predictions by −0.44 pp, 36.50% against 36.94%.
  • The explanation results describe two specific systems. They cover a strong member of the submitted system and the submitted eleven-model system itself, not skeleton emotion models in general.

The things that did not work are part of the record too. Supervised-contrastive heads, mixture-of-experts fusion, scenario-text alignment, vision-language distillation and calibration after training all failed or made results worse under this arrangement. The paper reports them with their configurations, since they are the reason the design went after disagreement between models instead of a better single model.

Terms

The words this page keeps because the paper and the talk use them.

ACII
The International Conference on Affective Computing and Intelligent Interaction. The 2026 edition, which hosts this challenge, is the fourteenth.
MMAC Challenge
The contest at ACII 2026 whose subject is cross-cultural emotion recognition from body movements. Entrants build a system that names the emotion in a motion capture recording, and a leaderboard the organisers run decides it.
DIEM-A
The Diverse Intercultural E-Motion Database of Asian Performers, the dataset the contest uses, published by Cheng, Tseng, Fujiwara, Schneider and Kitamura at ACII 2025.
Macro-F1
The score used throughout. It scores each of the twelve emotions separately and averages the twelve, so a system cannot do well by getting the common emotions right and ignoring the rare ones. It runs from 0 to 100%.
pp
Percentage points, the difference between two percentages. Going from 25.73% to 36.80% is a rise of 11.07 percentage points.
Leave-performers-out, and a fold
An arrangement in which the people in the test recordings never appear in the training recordings, so a system cannot succeed by learning how one particular person moves. Here the 74 training performers are split ten ways and the whole procedure runs ten times, each time holding a different group out. One such run is a fold.
Per-fold mean and pooled out-of-fold
Two ways of scoring the same ten runs. The per-fold mean scores each run and averages the ten, which is the headline 36.80 ± 4.00%. Pooling out-of-fold gathers the held-out predictions from all ten runs and scores them once, which gives 36.94%. They are different conventions on one system and should not be mixed.
Logit
A model's raw output score for each of the twelve emotions, before it is turned into a probability. The eleven models here are combined by averaging those raw scores, with nothing learned or tuned in the averaging.
STGCN++
The model the organisers supply as a reference point. It treats the skeleton as a graph, with joints as nodes and bones as edges.
Saliency
A score for how much a part of the body contributed to the model's answer, computed after the fact from the trained model. This page usually says the parts the model used instead.
Laban Movement Analysis
A vocabulary movement professionals use to describe how a body moves, in terms such as how much the head bows or how far the trunk leans. It is written by people, not learned from data.
Spearman ρ
A measure of whether two rankings agree, running from −1 for opposite orders through 0 for unrelated to +1 for identical orders.
Bootstrap confidence interval
A range for a number, found by drawing the data over and over with replacement and recomputing the number each time. Where the unit being redrawn matters, this page says which one it was: whole runs, whole performers, or whole emotions.
Permutation test
A p-value found by scrambling the data many times to see how often chance alone produces a result as strong as the real one. For the Laban comparison the region scores are shuffled within each emotion, which keeps the structure of the data intact.
