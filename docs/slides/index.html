Tested explanations

We test every claim about what the model reads, and the test can come back negative.

The task is to name one of twelve acted emotions from skeleton motion. There is no face, no audio and no scene, only how the body moves. The split is the hard part. Every test clip comes from a person the model never saw in training.

Chance is 8.3 percent, and a strong skeleton baseline only reaches about 25 percent. There is a lot of room above that, but the movement alone often does not settle which emotion it is.

There are two things I hope you take away. Ensembles win when their members make different mistakes, and you can test an explanation instead of only drawing it.

The setting is what makes this hard

Two people performing the same emotion differ about as much as one person performing two different emotions.

Close emotions move alike

Jealousy moves like contempt, and shame moves like guilt.

Every performer moves differently

Two things make it hard. First, emotions that are close in meaning are close in movement, so jealousy and contempt look alike, and so do shame and guilt.

Second, everyone has a personal movement style, so the same emotion performed by two people can vary about as much as two different emotions performed by one person.

Under leave-performers-out, our reproduced STGCN++ baseline reaches 25.73 percent Macro-F1, plus or minus about four. Every later comparison uses that number, because we reproduced it on exactly the same 74-performer split. A single better architecture does not gain much here.

What did not work

Under leave-performers-out, every obvious upgrade to a single model failed or made the score worse.

  • A supervised-contrastive head cost −11.53 pp.
  • Scenario-text alignment cost −1.42 pp, and its projection tracked performer identity.
  • A vision-language model ranked the true emotion last on skeletons with no face and no scene.
  • Post-hoc calibration moved the score by 0 to −0.14 pp.
30.05 ± 3.48% best single model the best any single model reached

All of these use the same 10-fold leave-performers-out protocol. The paper lists eight negative results, and they are why we stopped looking for one better architecture.

We did try the obvious upgrades to a single model. A supervised-contrastive head cost eleven and a half points, because twelve classes in a batch of 128 leave too few positives per class. Scenario-text alignment ended up encoding who the performer was instead of the emotion. Distillation from a vision-language model ranked the true emotion last on skeleton renderings with no face. Post-hoc calibration moved nothing.

The best single model we could build tops out at about 30 percent.

We report these on purpose. They are the reason we stopped looking for one better architecture and went for diversity instead.

Combine models that make different mistakes

We average the raw scores of four model families with different assumptions.

Each family assumes something different about how motion should be represented, so the families fail on different clips.

11 ensemble members four families
12.98 M trainable parameters 11 forward passes

We fit no fusion weights, so every member counts the same in the logit mean.

So the design is simple. We take four families that assume different things about motion: graph networks, an attention model, hybrid MLPs, and some frozen external pretraining. Then we average their raw pre-softmax scores. Every member counts the same, and there is nothing to fit.

There are eleven members and about thirteen million trainable parameters in total. Inference is eleven forward passes over a 64-frame clip, followed by a mean.

We are betting that the gain comes from the members disagreeing rather than from the extra parameters. The next three slides are where I try to show that the bet is right.

Explanations you can test

A heatmap cannot come back wrong. A test can come back negative.

The second half of the talk asks what the model reads, then checks every answer against the prediction.

That was the first half of the talk: where the number comes from and why it works.

The second half is the one I care more about. Most explainability work in this area draws a heatmap and asserts that it means something. A heatmap that means nothing looks the same as one that means something, so the picture can never be wrong.

We wanted claims that could come back negative, so we built the explanations as a set of checks, each one with a value and a null beside it. It is the same system, with no retraining. One of the checks does come back negative.

Six checks, five positive and one negative

When we say the model uses a body region, we change that region and check whether the prediction moves.

Everything after this depends on the first two checks.

The first is faithfulness. We rank the body parts by saliency, mask them in that order, and compare that against masking them in reverse order. The gap is positive, about 0.124, and it is positive in every one of the ten folds. At the finer 25-joint resolution it grows to 0.199.

The second is stability. Add noise to the input and the ranking barely moves, with a rank correlation of 0.98, and the top part never changes.

Without these two, the Laban alignment I am about to show would just be a picture agreeing with itself.

The negative we report

Saliency is flat from frame to frame, and concentrated on the head and neck across the body.

Across frames the important-versus-reverse AUC gap is about +0.002, against +0.124 ± 0.031 across body regions. Frame saliency entropy is 98.7% of log T per sample and 99.5% as a class mean. Order still matters: time reversal moves accuracy, so this is a claim about locating a frame.

This is the negative, and I think it is what makes the rest of this a test. When we ask which frames inside the window matter, the answer is all of them, roughly equally.

The saliency entropy is about 98.7 percent of the maximum, and the gap between important and reverse frames is close to zero, where the same measurement across body regions gave a clear positive. The head and neck dominate spatial saliency in all twelve emotions, so the evidence is spread through time and concentrated in space.

To be precise, this is about locating a frame, not about time in general. Order still matters, and reversing time does move accuracy. We report the null instead of hiding it.

One idea behind both halves

Performance varies about fourfold across performers, and the spread splits in two.

Half is intrinsic difficulty

Some performers are hard for every member.

Half is model-specific error

Averaging orthogonal members cancels this half. The explanations describe the same structure.

Where we are honest

  • Absolute Macro-F1 is low, so we claim a +43% relative gain over the baseline we reproduced.
  • The headline is ten-fold cross-validation on training performers, and the hidden test is pending.
  • Laban alignment is a match in vocabulary. Masking and motion edits test dependence separately.

We have not released the rationale dataset while consent and licensing are under review.

Let me put the two halves of the talk together. Performance varies by roughly a factor of four from one performer to the next. About half of that variation is difficulty every model shares, because some performers are hard for all of them. The other half is specific to one model: a performer that one architecture happens to fail on.

That second half is what averaging cancels, because the members fail on different people. It is also the same structure the explanations describe, so both halves of the talk come from one mechanism.

I want to be clear about the limits. The absolute score is low, so what I am claiming is a relative gain, forty-three percent over a baseline we reproduced on the same split. The headline comes from ten-fold cross-validation on the training performers, and the hidden test result is still pending. And the Laban alignment is a match in vocabulary rather than a proof of dependence. We test dependence separately, with masking and motion edits.
